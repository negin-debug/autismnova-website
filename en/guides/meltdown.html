Meltdowns: What Helps in the Moment and What Doesn't

A meltdown is not a tantrum, and it is not a parenting problem. Your child's nervous system is overloaded. In that moment, only safety and calm matter. Everything else comes later.

November 24, 2026 · 4 min read

In shortMake the world smaller, not your child: few words, little input, no demands. Keep everyone safe and stay close.

Meltdown or tantrum?

From the outside, they often look the same. The difference is whether your child is able to control what is happening right now.

Tantrum

  • has a goal: getting something or avoiding something
  • your child keeps an eye on how you react
  • usually stops once the goal is reached or clearly refused

Meltdown

  • happens involuntarily, your child cannot simply stop
  • your child is hard to reach, even with things they love
  • only stops once the nervous system has calmed down, whether or not your child got what they wanted

That is why rewards, punishment and bargaining don't help during a meltdown. All of them assume that your child is in control right now.

The quiet version: shutdown

Not every overload is loud. Some children go quiet and rigid, stop answering, lie down or hide. This is not sulking either. What helps is almost the same: don't question them, don't try to shake them out of it, turn the input down, be there in silence and give it time.

Six things that help in the moment

  1. Stay calm, your own breath first. Breathe out slowly three times and let your shoulders drop. Your calm reaches your child long before your words do.
  2. Keep everyone safe. Move dangerous things out of the way, close the door to the street, keep an eye on hard edges. Only hold your child if there is acute danger and no other way.
  3. Few words. One short sentence, always the same: “I'm here.” No questions, no explanations. Sentences hardly get through right now.
  4. Demand nothing. No “Calm down,” no “Look at me,” no “Come on now.” Every demand is one too many right now. Anything that can wait, waits.
  5. Turn down input and send onlookers away. Dim the lights, turn off the sound, kindly ask other people to leave. You change the surroundings, not your child.
  6. Offer closeness, don't force it. Stay within reach, sit calmly at eye level or lower. Touch only if your child seeks it.

What doesn't help

  • Explaining, bargaining, lecturing. None of it gets through during a meltdown.
  • “Stop it now!” or “Look at me!” That is one more demand nobody can meet right now.
  • Lots of questions like “What's wrong?” Answering is not possible in this moment.
  • Punishments or rewards for stopping. Your child cannot control it right now.
  • Onlookers and well-meant advice. Every extra person adds pressure.
  • Talking about it right away. There is time for that later.

Afterwards: recover first, talk later

When it is over, your child is exhausted. You probably are too.

  • Rest. Water, a blanket, a favorite thing, quiet. For you as well.
  • Plan a smaller day. After a meltdown, the capacity to cope is often lower for hours. Anything that doesn't have to happen today can be dropped.
  • Talk later, without blame. The conversation about why can wait: a few hours, sometimes until the next day. A sentence like “That was really hard for you earlier” is enough to start with.

For next time: prepare when things are calm

You can do the most before things tip over. It is worth preparing a few things while everything is calm.

  1. Know the early signs. Hands over the ears, fast breathing, repeating the same sentence, sudden silence. At this stage, calming supports can still work. In the middle of a meltdown, hardly at all.
  2. Write down triggers. Briefly note what happened before: noise, hunger, waiting, a change. After a few entries, patterns appear that you can't see in a single case.
  3. Choose a calming plan together. Three to six things that help your child, for example headphones, a stuffed animal or the quiet place. Practice them when things are calm, not only in an emergency.
  4. Let others know. Triggers, early signs, what helps and what to avoid: written down for grandparents, daycare or school.

This won't prevent every meltdown. But it helps you react earlier and not have to think when it matters.

When to get help

If your child hurts themselves or others, if meltdowns are very frequent or very long, or if your family doesn't know what to do anymore: talk to your pediatrician, an autism counseling service or a therapist. In acute danger, call your local emergency number (911 in the US, 999 in the UK, 112 in Europe).

How we know this

We always say how well something is supported by evidence. There is still little research on meltdowns.

PromisingCalm, supportive guidance by parents (co-regulation) was linked to less challenging behavior in a study of 51 families (Ting & Weiss 2017). There are no controlled studies of any specific approach.
Practice knowledgeFew words, less input, no demands: this is how many autism professionals and services work. The approach has not been tested in studies.
Lived experienceThe distinction between meltdown and shutdown comes mainly from autistic people themselves, for example from interviews with eight autistic children and young people (Phung et al. 2021). It is a description, not a diagnosis.

Sources: Ting, V. & Weiss, J. A. (2017): Emotion Regulation and Parent Co-Regulation in Children with Autism Spectrum Disorder. Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders 47, pages 680 to 689.
Phung, J., Penner, M., Pirlot, C. & Welch, C. (2021): What I Wish You Knew: Insights on Burnout, Inertia, Meltdown, and Shutdown From Autistic Youth. Frontiers in Psychology 12, 741421.
